# [Drone Resource Allocation] Collaborative Path Planning and Power Allocation for Hexagonal Connected Drones Based on Deep Reinforcement Learning - Apprentice-style Learning Implementation

1. Abstract: This paper investigates a joint path planning and power allocation mechanism for UAVs in sparse suburban environments, focusing on the perception of interference. The objective is to fly from an initial point to a destination using cellular units while ensuring required Quality of Service (QoS). Specifically, under the premise of considering both the shortest path and flight resource constraints, the UAV aims to maximize uplink throughput and minimize interference to ground user equipment (UE) connected to adjacent cell base stations (BS).
To train intelligent agents (i.e., UAVs), this study leverages expert knowledge to simulate scenarios and define desired behaviors. To address this issue, the paper adopts an apprenticeship learning approach based on Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) combined with Q-Learning and Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) techniques. By employing a supervised learning method, the performance of this approach is compared with a demonstration learning technique called Behavior Cloning (BC). Simulation and numerical results indicate that the proposed method can achieve expert-level performance. Moreover, the study confirms that, unlike BC technology, the proposed method's performance does not degrade in unfamiliar scenarios.
2. Introduction: The rapid development of UAV technology has sparked extensive research in both academia and industry. UAVs have been applied in various scenarios such as cargo transportation, search and rescue (SAR), aerial imaging, disaster monitoring, surveillance, and public safety [1-8]. These diverse applications fully utilize the unique capabilities of UAVs, including broad ground vision (FoV), three-dimensional mobility, rapid deployment capabilities, and flexible response abilities. Among these, UAV-assisted communication is an important research direction. In this area, UAVs can serve as airborne base stations (BS) or flying access points (AP) to provide connectivity support for ground user equipment (UE), particularly significant in disaster relief scenarios. UAVs can extend cellular network coverage, improve connection quality, and forward information between UEs and adjacent BSs, thereby enhancing overall communication infrastructure [9-14].
With the advancement of unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) technology, integrating it with cellular networks (including 5G and more advanced networks) presents numerous challenges such as interference management, spectrum management, quality of service (QoS) management, frequent handovers, and ensuring robustness, security, and reliability in communication [15-23]. Traditional cellular infrastructure is primarily designed for ground user equipment. To effectively serve airborne user equipment, significant improvements are necessary [24-27]. Advanced technologies such as massive multiple input multiple output (mMIMO) and beamforming algorithms have been proposed to enhance interference management and improve signal directionality precision. These methods aim to direct signals towards the target receiver, reducing interference to other users and ensuring reliable communication for airborne user equipment [28-30].
Most existing research adopts model-free reinforcement learning (RL) methods (such as temporal difference learning) to address complex issues like interference and path planning [31-46]. Although these methods have certain advantages, they often struggle in dynamic complex environments and encounter difficulties when dealing with non-convex and computationally intensive problems. The effectiveness of reinforcement learning largely depends on the definition of the reward function, which determines the relationship between the Markov decision process (MDP) environment, Q-value, and optimal policy learning. As problem complexity increases, defining comprehensive reward functions becomes increasingly challenging, leading to suboptimal policy performance. Moreover, in real scenarios, defining a reward function that encompasses all aspects of the problem is not easy, making comparative analysis with other methods lacking in persuasiveness.
Research Motivation: Due to the difficulty in constructing comprehensive reward functions, traditional reinforcement learning methods face significant challenges in dynamic complex environments, often resulting in suboptimal policy performance, especially when dealing with non-convex problems. Additionally, most existing methods fail to effectively leverage expert knowledge, which is crucial for developing more robust and adaptive UAV path planning and power allocation strategies.
As open-source software, emulators, and simulators (e.g., Network Simulator-3 (NS3) [47], srsLTE [48], OpenAirInterface [49] etc.) evolve, today, with expert assistance, optimal behavior data for agents can be generated in simulation environments. These data can be used by agents to learn optimal behavior through imitation learning (IL), demonstration learning (LfD), or apprenticeship learning (AL). In the literature [50], authors investigate disaster rescue scenarios and propose an imitation learning approach where drones assist ground user equipment in scheduling data packet transmissions to adjacent base stations. The authors implement a supervisory learning scheme using behavioral cloning (BC) to simulate the behavior of experts in similar scenarios.
Behavioral cloning generally does not consider any reward function, relying solely on visited states and actions, leading the agent to blindly imitate the expert's behavior. If the expert makes a wrong decision, the agent may replicate these errors. Moreover, if the agent visits a state that the expert has never encountered before, since this state is not included in the learned optimal strategy, the agent will not take appropriate actions.
To overcome the above limitations, this paper proposes a novel apprenticeship learning method based on reverse reinforcement learning (IRL) [51,52]. This method utilizes the data generated by the expert to reconstruct the optimal reward function, allowing the drone to learn effective strategies without relying on carefully defined reward functions. By employing deep Q-networks (DQN), the proposed method demonstrates outstanding performance across various scenarios (including those encountered during training). 3. Method Introduction
3.1 Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) Background
In the vast applications of artificial intelligence and robotics, ground robots, drones, and autonomous vehicles are among the intelligent agents operating in uncertain and complex environments. These agents often have to make decisions that are both complex and crucial.

In multi-objective reinforcement learning problems, intelligent agents need to handle multiple conflicting objective functions and complete various types of tasks. At this point, defining an optimal reward function that covers all objectives (10a)-(10i) is not easy. The problem addressed in this paper involves actions with diverse properties and behaviors such as mobility and power transmission levels, making it even more challenging for the agent to understand the reward function rationally. Typically, experienced experts within the field can demonstrate appropriate behaviors to the agent in such situations. These demonstrations not only save training time but also help the agent determine the optimal reward function based on optimal behaviors. This learning method is known as demonstrative learning (LfD) or instructional learning (PbD), also commonly referred to as model-based learning (MBL). Model-based learning technology is mainly divided into two categories: (i) behavioral cloning [69]; (ii) teacher-student learning based on inverse reinforcement learning [70,71].

In behavioral cloning, the agent blindly imitates the behavior of the expert (or teacher) without considering the optimality of the expert's actions. This means that if the expert makes a wrong decision, the agent may also make mistakes. Typically, the problem of behavioral cloning can be solved using supervised learning methods. However, if the state observed by the agent is something the expert has never encountered before, the learned strategy may not achieve optimal results. This issue can be resolved through data aggregation (DAGGER) methods [72], which aim to collect expert demonstrations based on the distribution of states observed by the agent. However, this method requires the expert to always be available, which is not always feasible across all scenarios and applications.
The study employs an inverse reinforcement learning-based apprenticeship learning approach to address the issues raised in Section 3.2, specifically:
1. The problem can be described as a (finite-state) Markov Decision Process (MDP)-formed sequential decision problem. In such MDP problems, a reward function is typically provided, which allows agents to determine optimal transition probabilities, value functions, and strategies, and use these optimal strategies to make decisions at each state. However, in complex system models, assuming that the reward function always exists is unrealistic, and manually obtaining the reward function poses numerous challenges.
2. By leveraging expert assistance, a series of trajectories and demonstrations can be obtained for learning optimal behavior. The experts could be virtual agents running on high-performance computers, but they cannot be directly deployed on drones. In this study, "trajectories" refer to sets of states and actions, rather than the trajectory motion or path of a drone. The goal of inverse reinforcement learning is to reconstruct an unknown reward function from expert demonstrations, and then apply this reward function to reinforcement learning methods to find the optimal strategy. However, this problem has "ill-posedness," as there may be multiple reward functions for a unique optimal strategy.
This study assumes that the experts are attempting to optimize an unknown reward function, which can be represented as a linear combination of known "features." 3.2.5 Imitation Learning: Behavior Cloning (BC)
This section introduces the models used in imitation learning (BC) within this paper. Typically, imitation learning models can be obtained through supervised learning tools to simulate expert behavior [86]. However, BC methods can only handle states observed by the expert; if the agent (or drone) encounters a state not seen by the expert, it may not obtain the optimal result.
To achieve imitation learning and behavior cloning, first collect all trajectories of the expert, which form the simulation dataset for training and testing evaluation. BC does not require the expert to continuously participate, but the data aggregation (DAGGER) method requires the expert to provide guidance on unvisited states. Due to the high cost and non-always feasible nature of the data aggregation method, this paper adopts the BC method for imitation learning. IV. Conclusion With the increasing application of drones, there is an increasingly urgent demand for reliable communication technologies that support low latency and high transmission rates. Cellular networks (especially 5G and more advanced networks) provide many advantages for drones, such as high reliability, wide coverage, and strong security. However, the increasing use of drones as airborne cellular users also brings unique challenges, such as potential interference with ground users and base stations.
This paper proposes a novel perception interference scheme that combines path planning and power allocation, suitable for autonomous cellular connections of drones (UAV). Drones need to fly from start points to destinations, during which they need to minimize interference with ground user equipment (UE) and maximize uplink throughput. To address this challenge, this paper proposes an apprenticeship learning method based on reverse reinforcement learning (IRL), which integrates Q-learning and deep Q network (DQN) solutions. At the same time, to conduct comparative analysis, a behavior cloning method based on decision tree supervision learning - behavior cloning (BC) was adopted.
Numerical simulation results show that the apprenticeship learning method based on reverse reinforcement learning can well fit expert behavior and perform better than the behavior cloning method. Moreover, users can control the degree of fit between the agent and the expert's expected behavior by defining thresholds. Importantly, the study confirms that the reverse reinforcement learning method remains effective even in scenarios where the system makes errors or the expert has not collected supervisory data for the visited states.
Furthermore, this study meets the key requirements of the UAV communication field, providing a solution that optimizes communication quality while reducing interference. By integrating cutting-edge technology from the reinforcement learning field, it offers valuable insights and methods for enhancing the reliability and performance of cellular connection drones, laying the foundation for improving drone applications and advancing the further development of autonomous aerial communication systems.
